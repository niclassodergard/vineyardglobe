## Overview
Madeira is a volcanic Portuguese island in the Atlantic, about 500 km west of Morocco. Its fortified wines were once shipped around the world, and they are famously long-lived. The island's steep terraces (poios) are tended by many small growers.

## Typical grapes
Tinta Negra is the most planted grape and underlies most everyday Madeira. The classic "noble" white grapes are Sercial (driest), Verdelho, Boal (Bual) and Malvasia (Malmsey, sweetest). A wine named after one of these varieties must contain at least 85% of it. Terrantez and Bastardo are rare.

## Wine styles
Labels state either a sweetness level, from dry to sweet, or the grape. Wines are fortified with grape spirit, and the level of sweetness depends on when fermentation is stopped. Age statements include 5, 10, 15 and 20 years old and older; Colheita is a single-vintage wine aged at least five years, while Frasqueira (Garrafeira) is a vintage wine aged at least 20 years in cask. Flavours range from citrus, nuts and smoke in dry styles to caramel, coffee and dried fruit in sweet ones, always with piercing acidity.

## What makes it special
Madeira is deliberately heated and oxidised. Cheaper wines go through estufagem, heated in tanks for at least three months, while the finest age slowly in casks in warm lofts for years, a process called canteiro. This makes the wine remarkably stable, so an opened bottle can last for months. High natural acidity balances the sweetness and gives remarkable longevity.
