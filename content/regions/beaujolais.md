## Overview
Beaujolais lies in eastern France between Mâcon in southern Burgundy and Lyon. Though often grouped with Burgundy, it has its own appellations and a very different grape focus.

## Typical grapes
Gamay (Gamay Noir à Jus Blanc) accounts for the vast majority of plantings. A little Chardonnay is grown for Beaujolais Blanc.

## Wine styles
Beaujolais and Beaujolais-Villages are juicy, light, red-fruited wines, and Beaujolais Nouveau is released on the third Thursday of November. Many wines use whole-bunch or semi-carbonic maceration, which emphasises bright fruit and low tannin. The ten Crus (Saint-Amour, Juliénas, Chénas, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie, Chiroubles, Morgon, Régnié, Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly) are more structured, and the best Moulin-à-Vent and Morgon can age for many years.

## What makes it special
The northern vineyards, home to the Crus and Beaujolais-Villages, stand largely on granite and other crystalline rocks such as schist and blue diorite, which suit Gamay and give the Crus their distinct characters, while the south has richer clay-limestone soils. Vines are traditionally trained as free-standing gobelet bushes, and harvesting is almost entirely by hand, because whole bunches are needed for the traditional semi-carbonic fermentation. Named lieux-dits, such as the Côte du Py in Morgon, are increasingly bottled separately, showing how much terroir variation exists within this one grape.
