## Overview
Tasmania is an island state south of mainland Australia and one of the country's coolest wine-growing regions. The whole island is a single Geographical Indication; vineyards are scattered across several small areas, mostly in the north around the Tamar Valley and Pipers River, on the east coast and in the south near Hobart. Total production is a very small share of Australia's wine.

## Typical grapes
Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are the most planted and underpin both sparkling and still wines. Riesling, Pinot Gris and Sauvignon Blanc are also grown, with Riesling showing lime and a fine, crisp acidity.

## Wine styles
Sparkling wines made by the traditional method from Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are a major strength, with high acidity and fine structure. Still Pinot Noir is typically light to medium in body, with red cherry and savoury notes, while Chardonnay is lean and citrusy. Riesling is usually dry to off-dry.

## What makes it special
Cool, maritime conditions, with Southern Ocean influence, mean slow ripening and naturally high acidity, which suits sparkling wine particularly well. Vineyards lie in the drier north and east of the island, sheltered from the very wet west by mountains. Frost and wind can reduce yields in some years. Mainland producers have long sourced Tasmanian fruit for premium sparkling wine, and in the 1980s Champagne Louis Roederer partnered with a Pipers River estate in the venture that became Jansz.
