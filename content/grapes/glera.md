## Overview
Glera is the white grape behind Prosecco, Italy's best-selling sparkling wine. It makes light, fragrant, fruit-driven wines that are meant to be drunk young.

## Origin and history
The grape was long called Prosecco, a name tied to the village of Prosecco near Trieste, though its true birthplace is debated. In 2009 Italy reorganised the denominations: Prosecco became the name of a protected wine origin rather than a grape, and the variety was officially renamed Glera, an old local synonym, so that producers elsewhere could no longer use Prosecco as a grape name. It is also known as Serprina in the Colli Euganei. The Prosecco DOC zone was widened across the Veneto and Friuli Venezia Giulia, with Conegliano Valdobbiadene and Asolo as the premium DOCGs.

## Flavour and structure
Glera is aromatic but delicate, with green apple, pear, white peach and acacia blossom, plus citrus and honeysuckle notes. Acidity is medium to high, alcohol is moderate and the body is light. The neutral, floral profile means site and yield strongly influence quality.

## Styles and ageing
Prosecco must contain at least 85 percent Glera, and most is made by the tank (Charmat or Martinotti) method, which preserves fresh fruit. It ranges from fully sparkling (spumante) to lightly fizzy (frizzante) and still. Sweetness runs from brut to extra dry, which is somewhat sweeter than brut despite its name. Some producers make cloudy, bottle-refermented versions left on their lees, known as col fondo. Nearly all wines are best within two to three years of the vintage, although top hillside bottlings can hold a little longer.
