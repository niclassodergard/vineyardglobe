## Overview
Muscat is not one grape but a large family of ancient varieties, prized for their unmistakable grapey, floral perfume. Berries come in white, pink and dark forms, but the wines most often seen are white, which is why it is listed here as a white grape.

## Origin and history
Muscats are among the oldest cultivated vines, and their exact birthplace is uncertain, probably somewhere in the eastern Mediterranean. DNA studies indicate that Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains (Muscat de Frontignan) is the family's key progenitor, and that Muscat of Alexandria, called Zibibbo in Sicily and Hanepoot in South Africa, is one of its offspring. Muscat Ottonel appeared at Angers in the Loire Valley in the mid-19th century, probably from a Chasselas parent. Muscat of Hamburg is a dark-berried relative, crossed from Schiava Grossa and Muscat of Alexandria.

## Flavour and structure
Muscat is one of the few wine grapes whose wines smell of fresh grapes. Expect orange blossom, rose, lychee, peach and citrus peel. Acidity is usually medium to medium-low, with tannin low and body light to medium. Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains is the most refined, Muscat of Alexandria is broader and more openly floral, and Muscat Ottonel is lighter and milder.

## Styles and ageing
Muscat makes an unusual range of wines: light, lightly sparkling Moscato d'Asti, dry Alsace Muscat, fortified Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise, and rich, sweet, fortified Rutherglen Muscat from a red-brown form of Muscat à Petits Grains, locally called Brown Muscat. Most dry and light styles are best young, while fortified sweet styles can age for decades.
