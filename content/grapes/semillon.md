## Overview
Sémillon is a white grape with a thin skin and a talent for developing waxy, honeyed complexity. It makes both some of the world's great sweet wines and long-lived dry whites.

## Origin and history
Sémillon is considered native to Bordeaux, where it is recorded in 1736 as "Sémillon de Saint-Émilion". It spread widely in the 19th century: by the 1820s it covered more than 90% of South Africa's vineyards, where it was called Groendruif or Wyndruif, and in Australia it was long sold as Hunter River Riesling. It is the main grape of Sauternes and Barsac, usually blended with a little Sauvignon Blanc, and is also blended with Sauvignon Blanc in dry white Bordeaux.

## Flavour and structure
Young Sémillon is relatively neutral, with citrus and green fruit and a rounded, medium-high body. Acidity is moderate, so site and picking date matter. With age or oak it takes on lanolin, toast, honey and nutty notes.

## Styles and ageing
Its thin skin is very susceptible to noble rot (Botrytis cinerea). In humid autumns this concentrates sugars and gives luscious sweet wines such as Sauternes, which can age for decades. In Australia's Hunter Valley, unoaked Sémillon is picked early to give low-alcohol, high-acid wines that look austere in youth. They develop toasty, honeyed flavours over many years in bottle. Oaked, richer dry styles are also made, notably in Bordeaux and Western Australia.
