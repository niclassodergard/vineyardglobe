## Overview
Aglianico is a late-ripening black grape of southern Italy, valued for deeply coloured, tannic and long-lived wines. It is the basis of Taurasi in Campania and Aglianico del Vulture in Basilicata, and is sometimes nicknamed the "Barolo of the South".

## Origin and history
Its origin is unclear. A popular story links it to ancient Greek settlers, and the name is often said to derive from "Ellenico", meaning Hellenic, but linguists doubt this, and some experts prefer a link with the Spanish "llano" (plain). The name first appears in print in 1520. The grape has long been grown in Campania and Basilicata, and it is sometimes linked, without certainty, to the Falernian wine of Roman times. Aglianicone is a closely related Campanian variety, not a clone.

## Flavour and structure
Aglianico shows black cherry, plum and blackberry, with dried herbs, tobacco, leather and smoky notes as it matures. Acidity and tannin are both high, and body is medium-high to full. Because it ripens very late, often into late October or November, it needs warm sites and long seasons. Volcanic soils, such as those around Mount Vulture, add firmness and a mineral edge.

## Styles and ageing
Most Aglianico is made as dry, structured red wine. Taurasi DOCG requires at least three years of ageing before release, including a year in wood, and the best examples are built for long cellaring. Quality bottlings can improve for 10 to 20 years or more, gaining complexity and softer tannins. Lighter, fruitier versions and some rosé and sparkling styles are also made, but serious expressions remain the grape's reputation.
