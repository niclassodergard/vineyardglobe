## Overview
Cinsault is a red grape of the Mediterranean, valued for light, fragrant, soft wines and for the fruit and perfume it adds to blends. It also copes well with heat and drought, which has renewed interest in it.

## Origin and history
Cinsault, officially spelled Cinsaut in France, is an old variety of southern France, possibly from the Hérault in the Languedoc, though its exact origin is uncertain. It is a classic ingredient in southern Rhône, Languedoc and Provence blends and in rosé, and in Puglia it is known as Ottavianello. It is not the same as the rare Languedoc variety Œillade Noire, despite long confusion between them. In South Africa it was called Hermitage, and in 1925 Abraham Perold crossed it with Pinot Noir to create Pinotage, whose name combines Pinot and Hermitage.

## Flavour and structure
Cinsault gives red cherry, strawberry, rose petal, dried herb and light spice. Tannin is low, acidity is medium, and body is light to medium, although high yields produce dilute wines. Low-yielding old bush vines give much more depth.

## Styles and ageing
It makes pale, dry rosé, particularly in Provence and the Languedoc, and is used in red blends such as Châteauneuf-du-Pape and in Lebanon's Château Musar. Modern producers in South Africa and Chile bottle old-vine Cinsault as a light, aromatic red. Most are best within a few years, though some old-vine wines age longer.
