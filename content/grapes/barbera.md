## Overview
Barbera is one of Italy's most widely planted red grapes and the everyday red of Piedmont. It is known for deep colour, bright acidity and low tannin, making a juicy, food-friendly wine.

## Origin and history
Barbera is generally believed to come from the Monferrato hills of Piedmont. Thirteenth-century lease records from Casale Monferrato and a 1514 document from Chieri are sometimes cited as early references, but they are open to interpretation, and the first clear description dates from 1798, by Count Nuvolone. For centuries it was regarded as a humble, reliable wine of the people. Its reputation rose from the 1980s, when producers such as Giacomo Bologna showed what low yields and barrique ageing could achieve. The revival survived the 1986 methanol scandal, in which cheap Barbera adulterated with methanol killed several people and badly damaged the grape's image.

## Flavour and structure
Barbera shows sour cherry, red plum and blackberry, often with violet and spice. Its hallmark is high acidity combined with low tannin and a medium body. Ripe, warmer sites give darker fruit and higher alcohol, while high yields give thin, tart wines.

## Styles and ageing
Barbera d'Asti and Barbera d'Alba are the benchmark appellations. Traditional versions are made in steel or large oak and drunk young and fresh. Richer styles use new small oak, which adds vanilla and softer texture, and can age for ten years or more. Its high acidity suits tomato-based and fatty dishes.
